      Concept · Life OS · revision six

      Ninety days,
      and the writing underneath.

      Revision five made the quarter readable at a glance: ninety days, seventeen subjects, a wall of marks. Revision six adds the layer that was missing — under every thing, and under every day, a real markdown page. The wall is still the map; the writing is what you actually needed to keep.

      This matters because of why the earlier tools died. Not the capture, not the filing — the stack of things that were half-written and never opened again. So the writing here is not a “details” drawer that eats what you type. It is printed back to you, formatted, the moment you stop editing.

      Seventeen is too many to hold in your head, and that is the original problem. The list is not a filing system; it is a portrait. Some of these subjects arrive on their own: payments fall due, school terms start, children have birthdays. Others only happen if you make room — sketches, prompts, a shelf of cartridges, a notebook. The first kind will find you. The second kind goes quiet without ever announcing it.

      Markdown, in two places

      • Under one thing A note that belongs to an entry. Open a thing in the day page and it has its own page: headings, bold and italic, lists, numbered steps, quotes, a table, a rule. The textarea is monospaced — which is what markdown is for, and honest about the fact that you are writing source. Switch to Read and it prints as a page.
      • Under a whole day Every unfolded day carries a space of its own, whether or not it has anything filed on it. That is where a day goes when it is not a list: what you decided, who you spoke to, what to do next. It is written in the same markdown, and it prints at the foot of the day page.
      • How you know there is writing Two small letters on the wall. MD means the day has its own page; N means one or more entries carry a note. They are type, not colour, so the wall's ink system is untouched — and both disappear when you are reading the quarter through a single subject.
      • Keeping a task from a note Tick it in the reading view, not the source. A - [ ] line prints as a real checkbox; clicking it rewrites the markdown underneath. The note stays a note — it never becomes a second task list competing with the wall.
      • What is deliberately not supported No HTML, no embedded files, no pasted images. A note is text that can be printed back safely. Everything is rendered node by node, never as markup, so nothing can be injected through a written note — and the syntax is the eight things a note actually needs.

      The syntax, in full

      A note accepts # heading, ## sub-heading, **bold**, *italic*, `code`, - list, 1. numbered list, - [ ] task, > quote, | table |, --- rule, [label](url) link. Two gestures exist only inside Horizon: @Name prints a person in citation type, and ^[day^what] prints a chip pointing at another day in the quarter — a note that refers forward without becoming a dependency.

      The index

      The band under the masthead is a printed index, not a filter menu. It prints a count in front of each subject — how many things sit in the next ninety days — and a dash where there is nothing. The dash is the point. It is not a warning, a badge, or a streak to protect; it is the same fact the wall already tells you about a quiet week, printed where you cannot avoid seeing it.

      Click one subject and the quarter is read through it. Every other mark leaves the page, the ink weight recomputes from what remains, and the gaps inside that subject become the shape — six weeks of nothing before the wedding, three payments in the same week. Click it again and the whole quarter returns. Nothing was filtered away; you simply looked closer. The day page still opens whole, because a day is never only one thing.

      What changed in six

      • Where writing lives Under the thing, and under the day. A plain one-line detail still belongs in the margins; the moment it needs structure, it becomes markdown — and it is printed back the way you wrote it.

      What changed in five

      • How a thing gets a subject The same way it gets a mark — you never file it. The words decide. dentist is Me, bond is Payments, sketches is Sketches. The composer prints the subject it will use while you type, a slash overrides it (/travels), and any entry can be moved from its own row in one click.
      • What the index measures Ninety days per subject, not a lifetime. A count if there is something ahead, a dash if there is not. No completion percentages, no red, no streak to keep alive.
      • Reading one subject The wall becomes that subject's quarter. Other marks leave the page, weight is recomputed from what remains, and the gaps are the reading. In the day page every entry still carries its own handle, so the territory is visible one level down.
      • How a day reads In stamps, not sentences. Each thing on a day becomes one mark in the cell. A day with four things shows four marks; a day with one shows it large. Density is count, not a number you have to interpret.
      • Who chooses the mark You never do. Type dentist and a cross appears; type bond and a coin. The composer shows the mark it will use while you are still typing, so the vocabulary teaches itself in the act of writing.
      • How you read an unfamiliar mark Hover it, and its words answer — in place, with the exact sentence you wrote and the subject it sits in. There is no key on the wall, and no legend to memorise before you can start.
      • What repetition buys Runs become visible. Eighteen marks, used over and over, turn the quarter into a pattern you recognise the way you recognise handwriting. A one-off is a mark; a habit is a row of them.
      • Where colour went Nowhere. Every mark is the same ink. Seventeen coloured subjects would shatter the palette and turn scanning into decoding; terracotta stays reserved for today, and ink coverage still carries weight.
      • When a mark fails Words are one click away. The masthead switch redraws the whole quarter in full sentences. The fold always prints the sentence, and a mark can be corrected on any single entry.

      The risk, stated plainly

      An unreadable mark is worse than a word, and an index of seventeen can become a chore. Three guards answer that. The vocabulary is small and closed — eighteen marks on one 24-unit grid with a single stroke weight, so they read as a printer's set rather than clip art. Every mark answers to its words on hover or focus. And nothing is ever asked twice: the sentence is in the fold, in the tooltip, in the composer, and one switch away on the wall; the subject is inferred, printed while you type, and changed in place if the guess is wrong.

      The wall still shows how much a day holds without a chart: an ordinary day is bare paper, a loaded day takes a band of wash, a landmark floods the block with ink and prints its numeral in terracotta. Marks sit inside that field. Weight in ink, kind in symbol, territory in the margin, words underneath.

      The seventeen

      In the order they were given, with the handle the app prints where space is tight. A subject has a name, a shorthand and a place in the list — and none of them is a colour.

          The four rules this app refuses to break

          • Nothing needs tending. There is no inbox, no tag tree, no streak. Marks and subjects are a byproduct of writing, not a filing decision you owe the app.
          • A blank day is a legal state. Open days are drawn as paper, not as unfinished work — and a subject with a dash is allowed to stay that way.
          • The full picture never leaves the screen. The quarter stays visible while a day is folded open, and a subject is read by narrowing the wall, never by leaving it.
          • One flourish. The wall is quiet paper, ink, and one terracotta. The only theatrical moment is a day unfolding to full size.

          Still open

          Three things this revision deliberately leaves undecided. Recurring things — a standing Tuesday still has to be typed each week. A subject that ends — the index has no way to say “closed”, only “quiet”. And a sharper one this revision introduces: the day after — a page written for a future day is a plan, and the same page read afterwards is a record; the app currently treats them as one object. A fourth, smaller: whether an entry's note should graduate into a page of its own when it outgrows its parent.
